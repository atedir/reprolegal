Surrogacy in Colombia: Legal Status, Costs and Key Steps

An overview of surrogacy in Colombia covering legal framework, typical costs, and the key considerations for intended parents researching this destination.

— · 20 October 2026

Why Intended Parents Are Looking at Colombia

Colombia has emerged as one of the more discussed Latin American destinations for surrogacy arrangements. Its combination of a relatively accessible healthcare infrastructure, a Spanish-speaking professional class with international experience, and proximity to North America has drawn attention from intended parents across several continents. As with any destination, however, a careful and methodical assessment is essential before any commitment is made.

This article provides a factual overview of the Colombian surrogacy landscape as it currently stands. It is not a recommendation, and it is not legal or medical advice. Circumstances change, and every individual case carries its own complexities.

The Legal Framework: What the Law Does and Does Not Say

Colombia does not have a dedicated surrogacy statute. As of writing, surrogacy arrangements are neither explicitly permitted nor explicitly prohibited by national legislation. The legal basis for most arrangements rests on a 1994 ruling by the Colombian Constitutional Court — Sentencia T-968 — which recognised the validity of altruistic surrogacy agreements under certain conditions, most notably that the surrogate acts without commercial incentive and that the intended parents have a medical justification for requiring a surrogate.

Because there is no comprehensive law, the practical application of that ruling varies considerably between individual courts, notaries, and civil registration offices. Parental recognition — that is, the process by which intended parents are recorded on a birth certificate — is not a guaranteed administrative step. In some cases it has required court proceedings; in others it has been handled through notarial declaration. Intended parents should not assume that the process will follow a single, predictable path.

Commercial surrogacy — arrangements involving payment beyond reasonable expenses — sits in a legally ambiguous position as of writing, and some Colombian legal practitioners advise strongly against structured payments that could be characterised as consideration for the surrogacy itself. Independent legal counsel in Colombia is not optional; it is a necessity.

Who Can Pursue Surrogacy in Colombia

Based on current practice rather than statute, Colombian surrogacy has most commonly been undertaken by:

  • Heterosexual couples where the intended mother cannot carry a pregnancy for documented medical reasons.
  • Single intended parents, though the process for single individuals carries additional legal complexity.
  • Same-sex couples, for whom the legal pathway is less clearly established as of writing and varies significantly by jurisdiction within Colombia.

Regardless of family structure, a medical opinion establishing the need for surrogacy is generally considered an important part of building a defensible legal record. Intended parents travelling internationally should also review how their home country will recognise any parentage order or birth certificate issued in Colombia. This is a distinct and equally important step.

Finding a Surrogate and the Matching Process

In line with the Constitutional Court's guidance, arrangements in Colombia are structured as altruistic. Surrogates are typically found through agency coordination, medical clinic networks, or personal introduction. The matching process generally involves psychological screening of the surrogate, medical evaluation, legal consultation with all parties, and the drafting of a surrogacy agreement.

The surrogacy agreement is an important document even though its enforceability under Colombian law has not been definitively settled by statute or a supreme court ruling. It serves to record the intentions of both parties, the scope of expenses covered, and the surrogate's acknowledgement that she does not intend to retain parental rights. Intended parents should have this document reviewed by independent Colombian counsel and, where possible, by legal counsel in their country of residence.

Surrogates are typically Colombian nationals residing in the country. Age and health criteria applied by reputable clinics are broadly consistent with international guidelines, though there is no nationally mandated standard as of writing.

The Medical Process

The medical pathway in Colombia follows the standard gestational surrogacy model: the intended parents' or donors' gametes are used to create an embryo via in vitro fertilisation, which is then transferred to the surrogate. Colombia has a number of established fertility clinics in cities including Bogotá and Medellín with equipment and laboratory standards broadly comparable to those found in Western Europe and North America, though standards vary and due diligence on individual clinics is strongly advised.

Success rates for IVF transfers depend on a wide range of factors including the age and health of the egg provider, embryo quality, and the surrogate's reproductive history. No outcome can be guaranteed. Intended parents should ask clinics for their own data and seek to understand how that data was collected and presented. Typical Costs

Colombia is generally considered a mid-range destination in global surrogacy terms. Costs vary considerably depending on the specific clinic, the legal pathway required, whether donor gametes are needed, and how many embryo transfer cycles are undertaken.

As a broad indication of typical ranges observed in the market:

  • IVF and embryo creation: typically in the range of USD 6,000 to USD 12,000 per cycle.
  • Surrogate compensation (expenses only, consistent with altruistic model): typically in the range of USD 10,000 to USD 20,000 across the full arrangement.
  • Legal fees in Colombia: typically USD 3,000 to USD 8,000 depending on complexity.
  • Agency coordination fees, where applicable: typically USD 5,000 to USD 15,000.
  • Ancillary costs (screening, medications, translation, travel, home-country legal work): variable and often underestimated.

Total all-in costs for a completed arrangement — from first consultation to birth — have typically ranged from USD 40,000 to USD 80,000, though cases outside this range are not uncommon. These figures are indicative only. For a more detailed breakdown of how surrogacy costs are structured internationally, see our costs guide.

Key Risks and Due Diligence Points

Intended parents considering Colombia should conduct thorough due diligence across the following areas:

  1. Legal recognition at home. Confirm with qualified counsel in your country of residence how a Colombian birth certificate and any Colombian court or notarial order will be treated. Some countries require additional proceedings; others may not recognise the arrangement at all as of writing.
  2. Citizenship and travel documents. Securing a passport or travel document for the child requires careful advance planning. Delays in documentation can result in extended stays in Colombia.
  3. Agency and clinic vetting. Colombia's surrogacy sector is not uniformly regulated. Ask for references, confirm clinic accreditation, and verify that any agency has a demonstrable track record.
  4. Contractual clarity. Ensure the surrogacy agreement is drafted and reviewed by independent counsel for each party. Shared representation is a conflict of interest.
  5. Contingency planning. Consider what happens in the event of a multiple pregnancy, a premature birth, a medical complication for the surrogate, or a dispute over parentage. These scenarios require financial and legal contingency arrangements made in advance.

Summary

Colombia offers a functioning surrogacy environment with experienced medical professionals and a legal basis rooted in constitutional court interpretation rather than statute. That distinction matters. The absence of a dedicated law means variability in how cases are handled, and intended parents must approach the process with careful preparation, independent legal advice in both Colombia and their home country, and realistic expectations about timelines, costs, and outcomes.
