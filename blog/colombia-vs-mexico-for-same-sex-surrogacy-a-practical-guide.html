Colombia vs Mexico for Same-Sex Surrogacy: A Practical Guide

A calm, factual comparison of Colombia and Mexico as surrogacy destinations for same-sex couples, covering law, cost, and process.

— · 13 September 2026

Introduction

For same-sex couples exploring surrogacy abroad, Latin America has become an increasingly considered region. Colombia and Mexico are two destinations that appear regularly in early research, and for understandable reasons: both countries have active surrogacy programmes, established fertility clinics, and legal environments that are, in relative terms, more accommodating than much of the world. However, the differences between them are significant enough to deserve careful, side-by-side analysis before any decision is made.

This article sets out the key considerations across legal framework, process, cost, and practical logistics. It does not constitute legal or medical advice, and every family's situation will carry its own variables.

Legal Framework: Colombia

Colombia does not have a specific surrogacy statute as of writing. Instead, surrogacy practice has developed through a series of Constitutional Court rulings, most notably decisions that recognised altruistic surrogacy arrangements and affirmed the rights of intended parents. Same-sex couples have benefited from Colombia's broader legal recognition of same-sex unions and, since 2016, equal marriage rights.

In practice, Colombian surrogacy arrangements are typically structured as altruistic agreements, meaning the surrogate receives no commercial fee beyond reasonable expenses and compensation for physical inconvenience. Courts have generally upheld parental orders for intended parents where genetic material from at least one parent is used, though outcomes are not uniform across all jurisdictions within the country. As of writing, the legal pathway remains case-law dependent rather than statute-based, and you should confirm the current position with Colombian legal counsel before proceeding.

A particular practical point is that birth registration in Colombia has become more consistently achievable for same-sex intended parents, particularly in Bogotá and Medellín, where notarial and civil registry offices have more experience with such applications. Smaller municipalities may present additional procedural complexity.

Legal Framework: Mexico

Mexico's legal landscape for surrogacy is notably fragmented because family law is governed at the state level rather than federally. This means the rules differ materially depending on which state the arrangement takes place in. As of writing, Tabasco and Sinaloa are among the states that have enacted specific surrogacy legislation, though the detail and accessibility of those laws have evolved over time and continue to do so.

Tabasco, historically the most-used state for international surrogacy, amended its Civil Code in 2016 to restrict surrogacy to Mexican nationals and residents, effectively closing it to most international intended parents. Sinaloa subsequently became a more frequently referenced option, with legislation that does not carry the same residency restriction, though legal practitioners advise that the court process can still be variable in practice. As of writing, same-sex couples have been able to complete surrogacy arrangements in certain Mexican states, but the legal route requires careful navigation and state-specific legal advice.

One notable advantage of Mexico for some couples is the availability of pre-birth orders in certain states, which can simplify the process of establishing parental status before the child is born. This is not universally available, and its reliability depends on the state and the presiding judge.

Comparing the Surrogate Recruitment Process

In both countries, surrogates are sourced through agencies or clinic networks. Reputable programmes will conduct medical, psychological, and background screening of candidates. The precise depth and standardisation of that screening varies by provider rather than by country, which underlines the importance of due diligence on any agency you engage.

In Colombia, the altruistic model means that financial arrangements with surrogates are more tightly framed. Intended parents should expect transparency about what is and is not permitted by way of compensation, and reputable agencies will provide clear contractual documentation. In Mexico, compensation structures vary by state and by agency, and intended parents should request a full breakdown before signing any agreement.

For same-sex male couples using a donated egg and gestational surrogate, both countries offer access to egg donation programmes, typically anonymous as of writing, though the regulatory basis for anonymity differs. Cost Considerations

Providing precise figures is not appropriate here because costs vary by clinic, agency, legal complexity, and individual medical factors. As a general orientation, total programme costs in Colombia typically range across a broad band depending on whether one or more IVF cycles are required, the extent of legal proceedings, and travel and accommodation. Mexico can present similar or somewhat higher costs in certain states, partly because legal proceedings can be lengthier and may require extended in-country stays.

In both destinations, intended parents should budget for the following categories:

  • Agency or coordination fees
  • Clinic and medical fees, including IVF cycles and prenatal care
  • Surrogate compensation and allowances
  • Legal fees in the destination country
  • Travel and accommodation
  • Home-country legal fees for parental recognition and passport applications

Nationality, Passports, and Return Home

Regardless of where the birth takes place, intended parents must navigate the process of obtaining travel documentation for the child and securing recognition of parentage in their home country. This process is entirely separate from the surrogacy arrangement itself and is governed by the laws of the parents' home jurisdiction.

Same-sex couples should take particular care to obtain advice from their home-country lawyer before committing to a destination, as recognition of parentage for both intended parents — particularly the non-genetically related parent — is handled differently across jurisdictions. Some countries require a second-parent adoption; others may recognise a foreign parental order directly. As of writing, no general rule applies, and the position is subject to change.

Colombian-born children acquire Colombian nationality at birth. Mexican-born children acquire Mexican nationality. In both cases, the child will require a passport from the destination country to travel, and intended parents will need to apply to their own country's embassy or relevant authority for documentation permitting the child to enter. Embassies can, and do, ask for supporting documentation relating to the surrogacy arrangement, and preparation of that file is something a reputable programme should assist with.

Practical and Logistical Factors

Both Colombia and Mexico are well served by international flights from Europe and North America. Bogotá and Mexico City both have significant international airports, and secondary cities such as Medellín or Guadalajara are accessible with one connection. Spanish is the primary language in both countries, and while most established clinics and agencies will have English-speaking coordinators, a working knowledge of Spanish or access to reliable translation support is advisable for day-to-day logistics.

Healthcare infrastructure in major cities in both countries is generally of a reasonable standard at private clinic level, though this varies considerably by institution. Time zone differences relative to Europe are significant (Colombia is UTC-5, most of Mexico UTC-6), which affects communication logistics for European intended parents.

Which Destination is More Suitable?

There is no universal answer. Colombia may suit couples who value a somewhat more developed body of case law supporting same-sex intended parents and a clear altruistic framework. Mexico may be preferable for couples whose home-country legal advisers have specific experience with Mexican parental orders, or where particular clinic relationships or genetic considerations make it the more practical choice.

The most important step is to consult qualified legal practitioners in both the destination country and your home country before selecting a programme. Summary

Colombia and Mexico each offer a viable, if legally complex, route to surrogacy for same-sex couples. Neither destination provides a straightforward or entirely predictable legal pathway as of writing. Careful preparation, qualified legal advice in all relevant jurisdictions, and thorough due diligence on any agency or clinic are essential steps before any commitment is made.
