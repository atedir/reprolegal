Surrogacy Laws: Mexico, Colombia and Canada Compared

A factual comparison of surrogacy laws in Mexico, Colombia and Canada to help intended parents understand key legal differences before proceeding.

— · 27 October 2026

Why Jurisdiction Matters Before Anything Else

For intended parents exploring surrogacy, the legal framework of the country in which the arrangement takes place will shape almost every other decision: who can participate, how parentage is established, what protections exist for all parties, and how a child's nationality and travel documents are secured. Mexico, Colombia and Canada represent three meaningfully different approaches to surrogacy law, and understanding those differences at the outset can prevent serious complications later.

This article sets out the broad legal landscape in each jurisdiction as of writing. Laws in this area change with some frequency, and intended parents should confirm the current position with qualified local counsel before making any commitments.

Mexico

A Fragmented Legal Landscape

Mexico does not have a single federal surrogacy law. Regulation is delegated to individual states, which means the legal environment varies considerably depending on where the arrangement is structured. As of writing, a small number of states have explicit legislative frameworks permitting altruistic or commercial surrogacy, while the majority have no specific surrogacy legislation at all, leaving arrangements to be governed — imperfectly — by general civil and contract law.

Tabasco was historically the most frequently used state for international surrogacy, but legislative amendments restricted access for foreign intended parents. Sinaloa subsequently introduced its own framework, and activity has shifted accordingly. The practical implication is that intended parents must identify not only that they are pursuing surrogacy in Mexico, but specifically in which state, and must understand the rules applicable in that state at the time of the arrangement.

Parentage and Birth Registration

Where a functioning legal framework exists, intended parents may be named on the birth certificate from the outset, which is a significant practical advantage. However, this is not uniform across states, and some arrangements require a post-birth court process to establish legal parentage. The position for single intended parents and same-sex couples varies by state and should be verified carefully.

Commercial vs Altruistic Surrogacy

Some Mexican states permit compensation to surrogates beyond reimbursement of expenses, making them effectively commercial jurisdictions, though terminology and permitted payments differ. Intended parents should obtain written legal advice on what payments are lawful in the specific state they are using, as this has implications both locally and in their home country.

Colombia

Judicial Recognition Without Legislation

Colombia presents a distinctive situation: it has no statute specifically governing surrogacy, yet its Constitutional Court has issued rulings that provide a degree of legal recognition. As of writing, the Constitutional Court has affirmed that surrogacy arrangements deserve legal protection and that intended parents have a pathway to establish parentage, but this is grounded in case law rather than codified legislation. The absence of a comprehensive statute introduces uncertainty, particularly as individual judges may interpret the existing rulings differently.

Who Can Access Surrogacy in Colombia

Colombian jurisprudence has generally addressed surrogacy in the context of heterosexual couples with a medical need, though as of writing there is movement toward broader recognition. Single intended parents and same-sex couples should take specific legal advice on their eligibility, as the position is not yet clearly settled by statute. This is an area where the law is evolving and local counsel is indispensable.

The Surrogacy Agreement

Because Colombia lacks dedicated legislation, the surrogacy agreement itself carries considerable weight as the primary document defining the relationship between the parties. Courts have looked to these agreements when resolving disputes. A carefully drafted contract, prepared by a Colombian lawyer with experience in reproductive law, is therefore not optional — it is a central pillar of the arrangement's legal integrity.

Parentage Orders

Establishing parentage in Colombia typically involves a judicial or administrative process following the birth. The surrogate may initially appear on the birth registration, with intended parents subsequently applying for a court order to transfer parentage. Timelines and procedural requirements vary, and delays in this process can complicate the child's travel documents, particularly for foreign intended parents seeking to return home. Intended parents should plan for this contingency when considering overall programme timelines.

Canada

A Permissive but Strictly Altruistic Framework

Canada is widely regarded as one of the more legally secure destinations for international surrogacy, largely because it has federal legislation — the Assisted Human Reproduction Act — that sets a national framework. As of writing, commercial surrogacy is prohibited under federal law; surrogates may be reimbursed for documented expenses related to the pregnancy, but payment beyond that is not lawful. This places Canada firmly in the altruistic category.

The practical consequence is that finding a surrogate in Canada can take longer than in jurisdictions where financial compensation is permitted, and intended parents should factor this into their expectations regarding programme timelines.

Provincial Variation in Parentage Law

While the federal Act governs payment, parentage law in Canada is a provincial matter. Several provinces, including British Columbia and Ontario, have modernised their legislation to allow pre-birth parentage orders, meaning intended parents can be recognised as legal parents before the child is born. Other provinces still require post-birth court applications. The province in which the surrogate resides will therefore determine the parentage process, and this is a material factor in programme planning.

Access for International Intended Parents

Canada does not restrict surrogacy to Canadian citizens or residents. International intended parents can pursue surrogacy in Canada, though they must be attentive to immigration requirements when bringing a child born in Canada to their home country. A child born in Canada to a Canadian surrogate is generally entitled to Canadian citizenship, which can be advantageous but also introduces complexity depending on the intended parents' home jurisdiction. Home country immigration counsel should be engaged well in advance of the birth.

Legal Security and Dispute Resolution

Canada's established legal system, including its judiciary's familiarity with assisted reproduction matters and the availability of pre-birth orders in certain provinces, means that the legal risk of a disputed outcome, while never zero, is generally considered lower than in jurisdictions that lack legislative frameworks. That said, no surrogacy arrangement anywhere is entirely without legal risk, and intended parents should not proceed without independent legal representation.

A Side-by-Side Summary

  • Mexico: State-level regulation; varies significantly by state; some states permit commercial surrogacy; pre-birth parentage possible in certain states.
  • Colombia: No dedicated statute; case law provides partial recognition; altruistic in practice; post-birth parentage process typical; evolving position for non-heterosexual couples.
  • Canada: Federal prohibition on commercial surrogacy; altruistic only; provincial variation in parentage law; pre-birth orders available in several provinces; accessible to international intended parents.

Practical Considerations for Intended Parents

Choosing between these three jurisdictions should not be driven by cost alone, though cost is a legitimate consideration. The strength of the legal framework, the realistic timeline to secure parentage, and the implications for the child's nationality and travel documents all carry significant weight. Intended parents should also consider the legal position in their home country: some jurisdictions refuse to recognise foreign surrogacy orders or impose additional requirements before recognising parentage established abroad.

A common and avoidable mistake is to focus exclusively on the destination country's law while neglecting the home country dimension. Engaging family law counsel in both jurisdictions simultaneously, from an early stage, substantially reduces the risk of a child being stranded or a parentage order being challenged on return.

Conclusion

Mexico, Colombia and Canada each offer a workable path to surrogacy for international intended parents, but they differ materially in the security, predictability and accessibility of their legal frameworks. None is without complexity. Thorough due diligence, jurisdiction-specific legal advice, and a realistic understanding of timelines are the foundations of a well-managed arrangement.
