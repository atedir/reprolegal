Recognising a Surrogacy-Born Child in Australia: Legal Steps

Australian intended parents face specific legal steps to gain parentage after surrogacy abroad or at home. Here is what to expect.

— · 6 October 2026

Why Legal Recognition Matters After Surrogacy

Completing a surrogacy arrangement — whether in Australia or overseas — does not automatically make the intended parents the legal parents of the child in Australian law. The birth registration in the country or territory where the child is born will typically name the surrogate (and sometimes her spouse or partner) as the legal parent or parents. Until an Australian court or registry process formally recognises the intended parents, they may lack the legal authority to make decisions about the child's healthcare, education, and welfare, and the child's citizenship or passport status may be uncertain. Taking the correct steps promptly is essential.

Domestic Surrogacy: The Parentage Order Pathway

Australia permits altruistic surrogacy. Commercial surrogacy — where the surrogate receives payment beyond reasonable expenses — is prohibited in most Australian states and territories, and engaging in commercial surrogacy overseas is a criminal offence under the laws of several jurisdictions, including Queensland, New South Wales, and the Australian Capital Territory, as of writing. Intended parents should confirm the current position with local counsel before proceeding.

Applying for a Parentage Order

Each Australian state and territory has its own legislation governing parentage orders. In broad terms, intended parents may apply to the relevant court — typically the Supreme Court or the Family Court of the relevant jurisdiction — for an order that transfers legal parentage from the surrogate (and her partner, if applicable) to the intended parents. The application is generally made after the child is born and after a minimum waiting period, which varies by jurisdiction but is commonly four weeks.

The court will ordinarily require evidence of the following before granting a parentage order:

  • That the surrogacy arrangement was entered into before conception and was documented in writing.
  • That the surrogate and any partner consent to the order, freely and without duress, after the child's birth.
  • That the surrogacy was altruistic and that no prohibited payments were made.
  • That at least one intended parent is genetically related to the child (a requirement in some, though not all, jurisdictions as of writing).
  • That the making of the order is in the best interests of the child.

The court may appoint an independent children's lawyer and may require reports from social workers or counsellors. Once a parentage order is granted, the child's birth registration is amended to reflect the intended parents as the legal parents.

International Surrogacy: A More Complex Path

Many Australian intended parents travel abroad for surrogacy, often to destinations with established programmes. The legal recognition process on return to Australia involves two distinct stages: securing the child's right to enter Australia, and then obtaining legal parentage within Australia.

Stage One: Bringing the Child Home

A child born overseas through surrogacy may be an Australian citizen by descent if at least one intended parent is an Australian citizen and that parent is the child's biological parent. In that case, the parents may apply to the Department of Home Affairs for citizenship by descent and then for an Australian passport. If neither intended parent is the biological parent, or if citizenship by descent cannot be established, the child may require a visa to enter Australia. The specific visa pathway depends on the child's country of birth and the parents' circumstances. Expert immigration advice is strongly recommended at the earliest stage of planning. Further information on country-specific considerations is available on our destinations and countries page.

Stage Two: Obtaining Parentage Recognition in Australia

Securing entry to Australia does not resolve legal parentage. Under the Family Law Act 1975 (Cth), as of writing, there is no dedicated federal mechanism for recognising a foreign surrogacy-determined parentage order. The Family Court of Australia (now the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia) has, in a number of cases, made parenting orders that achieve a practical outcome for the family, but these are distinct from parentage orders and do not amend the birth register in the same way.

Some states and territories provide a pathway for intended parents who have completed an overseas surrogacy to apply for a parentage order domestically, provided the arrangement meets local requirements — including that it would have been permissible had it taken place in that jurisdiction. This is a nuanced area of law and the requirements differ significantly between states. Intended parents should obtain specialist family law advice before travelling and again on return.

Documents typically required for domestic court proceedings following overseas surrogacy include:

  • The child's overseas birth certificate (with certified translation if not in English).
  • The surrogacy agreement (and any translations required).
  • Evidence of genetic parentage, usually in the form of a DNA report from an accredited laboratory.
  • Consent documentation from the surrogate and her partner.
  • Evidence of the child's immigration status in Australia.
  • Affidavits from the intended parents and, often, the surrogate.

Citizenship, Passports and Identity Documents

Once parentage is legally established and, where necessary, the birth registration amended, the intended parents can ensure the child holds correct identity documents. For children recognised as Australian citizens, a passport application may be made in the usual way. Where the child is not an Australian citizen, the family may wish to explore naturalisation or permanent residency pathways. These are immigration matters outside the scope of a surrogacy parentage application and require separate advice.

Timeline and Costs: What to Expect

The time required to obtain a parentage order varies. For domestic surrogacy arrangements, the process from application to final order typically takes several months, though this depends on the court's workload and the complexity of the case. For overseas surrogacy, the combined immigration and parentage process may extend to twelve months or longer in some circumstances.

Legal costs for parentage order applications vary considerably by jurisdiction and complexity. As a general indication, legal fees for a straightforward domestic case are typically in the range of several thousand to tens of thousands of Australian dollars. Overseas surrogacy cases involving additional immigration work will attract additional costs. Our costs and budgeting guidance provides a broader framework for financial planning across the surrogacy journey.

Practical Steps Before and After the Birth

Preparation significantly reduces the risk of delays or complications. Intended parents should consider the following at each stage:

  1. Before entering any surrogacy arrangement: Obtain legal advice in the jurisdiction where the birth will occur and in the Australian state or territory where you reside. Confirm that the arrangement is lawful in both places.
  2. Before the birth: Prepare all surrogacy agreement documentation carefully. Where overseas, engage a local lawyer experienced in the surrogacy laws of that country. See our how it works section for an overview of the full process.
  3. Immediately after the birth: Obtain the birth certificate and any exit documentation required by the birth country. Begin the citizenship or visa application without delay.
  4. On return to Australia: Instruct an Australian family lawyer promptly to advise on the parentage order process in your state or territory.
