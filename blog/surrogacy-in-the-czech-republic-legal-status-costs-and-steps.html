Surrogacy in the Czech Republic: Legal Status, Costs and Steps

An overview of surrogacy in the Czech Republic covering legal status, typical costs, and the key steps intended parents should follow.

— · 17 November 2026

Surrogacy in the Czech Republic: What Intended Parents Need to Know

The Czech Republic occupies an unusual position in the European surrogacy landscape. It is neither a country with a comprehensive surrogacy statute nor one that explicitly prohibits the practice. As of writing, Czech law neither regulates nor expressly bans altruistic surrogacy arrangements, which means that programmes do operate, but always within a framework of significant legal ambiguity. Intended parents considering this destination must approach it with careful due diligence and specialist legal counsel both locally and in their home jurisdiction.

This article provides a factual overview of the current situation. It is not medical or legal advice, and all details should be verified with qualified professionals before any decisions are made.

The Legal Landscape

No Dedicated Surrogacy Legislation

As of writing, the Czech Republic has no specific statute governing surrogacy contracts, parental transfer or the rights and obligations of surrogates. The Civil Code and the Act on Specific Health Services contain provisions relevant to assisted reproduction, but these do not create a clear legal pathway for surrogacy. Because the law does not explicitly prohibit altruistic arrangements, some clinics and legal practitioners have developed working protocols, but these remain untested by any definitive court ruling at the highest level.

Surrogacy contracts as such are not legally enforceable in Czech courts as of writing. This is a critical point: a written agreement between intended parents and a surrogate cannot be directly relied upon to compel compliance by either party. The arrangement therefore depends heavily on mutual trust, thorough screening, and careful coordination by an experienced agency.

Parentage and Birth Registration

Czech law follows the principle of mater semper certa est — the woman who gives birth is the legal mother. This means that upon delivery, the surrogate is recorded as the child's mother on the birth certificate. The intended father, if genetically related to the child, may be able to establish paternity, but the intended mother has no automatic legal route to parentage. As of writing, a legal process — which typically involves the surrogate and her spouse or partner (if applicable) relinquishing parental rights, followed by adoption or a parental order equivalent — is required to transfer legal parentage to both intended parents. The precise mechanism available will depend on individual circumstances and must be confirmed with local Czech legal counsel.

International Implications

Intended parents who are not Czech nationals face an additional layer of complexity. Many countries, including several EU member states, do not automatically recognise parental orders or adoptions granted abroad. Before committing to a Czech programme, it is essential to obtain a written legal opinion from a qualified lawyer in your home country confirming how the child's nationality and your parental status will be treated upon return. Who Can Pursue Surrogacy in the Czech Republic

As of writing, clinics operating in the Czech Republic generally require intended parents to be in a heterosexual partnership and to have a documented medical indication for surrogacy — that is, a clinical reason why the intended mother cannot carry a pregnancy herself. Eligibility criteria vary between clinics, and same-sex couples or single intended parents face a more restricted pathway given the absence of a legal framework that accommodates such arrangements. Specific eligibility should be discussed directly with the clinic and a local legal adviser.

Typical Costs

Costs in the Czech Republic are generally lower than in the United States or Canada, though they remain considerable when all elements are accounted for. The following ranges are typical at the time of writing and should be treated as illustrative only; actual figures will vary depending on the clinic, the specific services required, and individual circumstances.

  • IVF and embryo creation: typically €4,000–€8,000 per cycle, depending on the protocol and use of donor eggs or sperm.
  • Surrogate compensation and expenses: Czech programmes are altruistic in character, meaning surrogates may not receive a commercial fee. Reasonable pregnancy-related expenses — travel, maternity clothing, lost earnings — are typically reimbursed. Total surrogate-related costs often fall in the range of €8,000–€15,000.
  • Agency coordination fees: typically €5,000–€12,000, covering matching, case management and support.
  • Legal fees (Czech): typically €3,000–€7,000, covering the surrogacy agreement, parental order process and birth registration.
  • Legal fees (home country): variable; budgeting a minimum of €2,000–€5,000 is prudent, though complex cases may cost significantly more.
  • Miscellaneous (travel, translation, psychological screening, insurance): typically €2,000–€5,000.

All-in, intended parents should anticipate a total investment in the range of €25,000–€50,000, though costs can exceed this range if multiple IVF cycles are required or legal proceedings become protracted. For a broader cost comparison across destinations, see our costs guide.

Key Steps in a Czech Surrogacy Programme

Step 1: Initial Consultation and Medical Assessment

The process typically begins with a consultation with a Czech fertility clinic, at which the intended parents' medical history is reviewed and a treatment protocol is proposed. If egg donation is required, a donor is selected from the clinic's pool. Czech law permits anonymous egg donation as of writing.

Step 2: Agency Matching and Surrogate Screening

An agency coordinates the identification of a potential surrogate. Thorough psychological, medical and background screening is conducted before any match is proposed. Intended parents and the surrogate typically meet before proceeding. This stage can take several months.

Step 3: Legal Agreements

Czech legal counsel drafts an agreement setting out the expectations, expense reimbursements and intentions of all parties. As noted above, this agreement is not directly enforceable in the same way as a contract in a fully regulated jurisdiction, but it is nonetheless important as evidence of intent and mutual understanding.

Step 4: Embryo Transfer and Pregnancy

Once legal agreements are in place and all medical screening is complete, the embryo transfer proceeds. The surrogate's pregnancy is monitored by the clinic. Regular communication between the agency, the surrogate and the intended parents is maintained throughout. No clinical outcome can be guaranteed at any stage of this process.

Step 5: Birth and Parental Order Process

Following the birth, the surrogate is registered as the legal mother. The legal process to transfer parentage begins promptly. The intended father's paternity is typically established first, followed by the process to establish the intended mother's legal status. This process can take several months and requires the active cooperation of the surrogate. Intended parents should not make travel or return arrangements on the assumption that this will conclude within a fixed timeframe.

Step 6: Home Country Recognition

Once Czech parental documentation is obtained, intended parents work with home-country lawyers to have the child's status and their parental rights recognised domestically. This step is jurisdiction-dependent and can range from relatively straightforward to complex. Early legal advice in the home country is strongly recommended — ideally sought before embryo transfer takes place. Our how it works guide explains the broader journey in more detail.

Choosing an Agency and Clinic

Given the absence of a regulatory framework, the quality and ethics of the agency and clinic are paramount. Intended parents should request full documentation of the agency's matching and screening procedures, ask for references from previous clients, and ensure their legal representatives are independent from the clinic and agency. Avoid arrangements in which a single organisation controls all elements of the process without independent oversight.

Summary

Surrogacy in the Czech Republic is neither clearly legal nor clearly prohibited as of writing. Programmes do operate, and costs are relatively modest by Western European standards, but the legal ambiguity surrounding parental transfer means that careful preparation — including independent legal advice in both the Czech Republic and your home country — is not optional. Intended parents who proceed without this preparation risk significant difficulties establishing their parental status, both locally and at home.
