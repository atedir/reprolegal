Surrogacy in Georgia: what changed for foreign intended parents

Georgia tightened its surrogacy rules for foreigners. Here is what intended parents need to know about eligibility, legal process and next steps.

— · 12 August 2026 · 12 August 2026 · 7 min

Why Georgia became a popular surrogacy destination

For roughly two decades, Georgia attracted intended parents from across Europe, North America and beyond. The country offered a permissive legal framework, relatively low typical costs, and a well-established network of fertility clinics and surrogacy agencies. Under Georgian law, surrogacy has long been legally recognised and the birth certificate issued to intended parents did not name the surrogate mother, simplifying the process of establishing parental rights from the outset.

Those features made Georgia one of the more accessible destinations for international surrogacy at a time when many other jurisdictions were closing or restricting access. That accessibility, however, has narrowed considerably in recent years.

What the 2024 legislative changes introduced

As of writing, Georgian law restricts access to surrogacy for foreign nationals in ways that were not in place prior to the amendments introduced in late 2023 and enacted in 2024. The core change is that surrogacy arrangements in Georgia are now, as of writing, limited to married heterosexual couples where both partners are citizens of countries that legally recognise surrogacy. Same-sex couples, single intended parents and unmarried couples are no longer eligible to pursue surrogacy in Georgia under the revised framework, regardless of the legal position in their home country.

It is important to note that the detail and application of these rules continue to evolve, and the legislative environment in Georgia remains subject to further revision. Intended parents should confirm the current position with qualified local legal counsel before making any decisions or commitments.

The citizenship and recognition requirement

The requirement that intended parents hold citizenship of a country where surrogacy is legally recognised introduces an additional layer of complexity. Many European citizens, for example, come from countries where surrogacy is prohibited or sits in a legal grey area. For those individuals, access to Georgia as a destination may be effectively closed, even if they are married and meet other eligibility criteria.

Confirming whether your home country legally recognises surrogacy is not always straightforward. Laws vary not only by country but sometimes by region, and the interaction between your home country's family law and Georgian eligibility rules requires careful legal analysis. ReproLegal always recommends obtaining a written opinion from counsel qualified in both Georgian law and your home country's family law before proceeding.

What has not changed

Several features of the Georgian framework remain intact, as of writing. The legal parentage structure — whereby intended parents are named on the birth certificate and the surrogate has no parental claim — continues to apply to eligible parties. Georgian law still permits compensated surrogacy arrangements, meaning surrogates may receive financial support beyond medical expenses, within limits set by the agreement. The health and psychological screening requirements for surrogates also remain in place and are enforced by clinics operating under Georgian medical licensing rules.

Practical implications for the matching and legal process

For those who do meet the revised eligibility criteria, the practical steps in a Georgian surrogacy arrangement broadly follow a pattern familiar from other regulated destinations. A simplified outline appears below, though individual circumstances will vary.

  1. Eligibility confirmation: obtaining legal opinions on both Georgian eligibility and home country recognition of the anticipated birth certificate and parental order.
  2. Programme enrolment: engaging a licensed Georgian agency or clinic programme and completing the required documentation, including proof of marriage and citizenship.
  3. Medical assessment: both intended parents and the prospective surrogate undergo medical and psychological screening. The outcome of this process cannot be guaranteed in advance.
  4. Matching: the agency proposes a surrogate match based on medical compatibility, preferences and availability. Timelines for matching vary and are not fixed.
  5. Legal agreement: a surrogacy contract is drafted and reviewed by Georgian-qualified lawyers. The contract must be in place before any medical procedures commence.
  6. IVF and embryo transfer: the fertility treatment cycle begins. Success rates depend on individual clinical factors and cannot be predicted with certainty.
  7. Birth registration: upon birth, intended parents are registered as legal parents on the Georgian birth certificate, subject to the legal framework remaining as described at the time of birth.
  8. Exit documentation: obtaining a passport and any necessary visa for the child to travel to the intended parents' home country. This step requires early legal planning, as some countries apply specific entry requirements to children born via surrogacy abroad.

Cost considerations under the revised framework

The regulatory changes have introduced some additional administrative costs, principally around legal documentation, certified translations and apostille requirements. Overall, Georgia continues to sit at the lower end of typical international surrogacy cost ranges. A complete programme — including agency fees, surrogate compensation, legal fees, clinic fees and basic travel — typically falls within a range that is broadly lower than comparable programmes in, for example, the United States or Canada. However, costs vary considerably depending on the clinic chosen, the number of transfer cycles required and the complexity of the legal work in both countries.

It would be misleading to quote a single figure, as individual circumstances differ significantly. Our costs guide sets out the main cost categories and provides indicative ranges to help intended parents plan financially.

Assessing Georgia against other destinations

Given the restrictions now in place, Georgia is no longer a viable route for a significant proportion of intended parents who previously might have considered it. Same-sex couples, single parents and those from countries where surrogacy is not legally recognised will need to look elsewhere.

Destinations that currently maintain broader access for international intended parents include, as of writing, certain states within the United States, Canada, Colombia and a small number of others. Each carries its own legal framework, cost profile and practical considerations. Our countries guide provides a comparative overview to help intended parents identify which destinations are most relevant to their situation.

Due diligence remains essential

Regardless of destination, the due diligence process does not change. Intended parents should verify that any agency or clinic they engage holds current relevant licences, that surrogacy contracts are reviewed by independent counsel rather than solely by agency-appointed lawyers, and that exit documentation planning begins well before the anticipated birth date. The legislative environment in Georgia, as in a number of other jurisdictions, has demonstrated that rules can change with relatively short notice, and contingency planning forms part of responsible programme management.

Next steps for intended parents considering Georgia

If you are considering Georgia and believe you meet the current eligibility criteria, the appropriate first step is a legal eligibility review rather than a clinical assessment. Establishing that you can lawfully pursue a surrogacy arrangement in Georgia, and that the resulting birth certificate and parental status will be recognised in your home country, must precede any medical or agency engagement.
