Surrogacy in Albania: Legal Framework, Costs and Practical Steps

An overview of surrogacy in Albania covering the legal status, typical costs, and the practical steps intended parents should consider before proceeding.

— · 24 November 2026

Surrogacy in Albania: What Intended Parents Need to Know

Albania has attracted growing interest from intended parents seeking surrogacy arrangements in south-eastern Europe. Its relatively low cost of living, geographic proximity to Western Europe, and an evolving regulatory environment have placed it on the radar of those researching international options. However, the legal landscape remains fluid, and careful due diligence is essential before committing to any programme.

This article sets out the current state of the legal framework, the costs you can typically expect, and the practical steps involved in pursuing surrogacy in Albania. It is intended as a general orientation, not a substitute for personalised legal or medical advice.

The Legal Framework

Current Status of Surrogacy Law

As of writing, Albania does not have a comprehensive, dedicated statute governing surrogacy. Reproductive matters are addressed in part through the Law on Reproductive Health and through provisions of the Family Code, but neither instrument provides an explicit, detailed framework for gestational surrogacy arrangements. This absence of clear legislation creates both ambiguity and risk for intended parents and surrogates alike.

In practice, some agencies and lawyers operating in Albania have proceeded on the basis that gestational surrogacy is not explicitly prohibited, treating the legal silence as a permissive gap. This interpretation is contested, however, and as of writing there is no binding judicial precedent at a national level that definitively confirms or denies the enforceability of surrogacy contracts. Intended parents should confirm the current position with qualified Albanian legal counsel before proceeding, as the regulatory environment may have changed since this article was written.

Parentage and Birth Registration

Establishing legal parentage is one of the most critical steps in any surrogacy arrangement. Under Albanian family law, as of writing, the woman who gives birth is typically recorded as the legal mother. This means that an intended mother would not automatically appear on the birth certificate, and a formal legal process — which may involve a court order or an adoption-style procedure — is likely to be required to transfer or confirm parental rights.

For the intended father, particularly where donor sperm is not involved, a DNA-based paternity acknowledgement may assist with establishing a biological link, but this does not resolve the question of legal parentage on its own. The precise procedural route will depend on the specifics of each case and should be mapped out with local counsel well in advance of the birth.

Recognition in Your Home Country

Even if an arrangement proceeds smoothly in Albania, intended parents must consider how the parentage order or birth certificate will be recognised in their country of residence. Many European jurisdictions apply their own rules when determining whether a foreign surrogacy arrangement confers parental status. Some countries require a domestic court application; others may refuse recognition entirely. You can find a broader comparison of destination-specific considerations on our countries overview page.

Eligibility and Matching

Who Can Pursue Surrogacy in Albania

As of writing, there are no statutory eligibility criteria for intended parents set out in Albanian law. Agencies operating in the country have typically worked with heterosexual married couples, though the position for single intended parents or same-sex couples is legally uncertain and carries additional risk. Same-sex relationships do not have formal legal recognition in Albania as of writing, which creates practical complications around birth registration and parental rights.

Surrogate Eligibility

In the absence of a dedicated surrogacy statute, there are no codified criteria governing who may act as a surrogate in Albania. Responsible agencies will ordinarily apply their own screening protocols, which typically include psychological assessment, a full medical evaluation, evidence of prior successful pregnancy, and an assessment of financial independence. Intended parents should ask any agency they approach to provide written details of their screening standards and to share anonymised outcomes from previous matches.

Typical Costs

Albania is generally positioned as a more affordable destination than Western European countries or the United States. However, intended parents should approach headline figures with caution, as total programme costs depend on a wide range of variables. Our costs guide provides a fuller breakdown of the components that typically make up an international surrogacy budget.

Indicative Cost Ranges

  • Agency coordination fee: typically in the range of €8,000 to €18,000, depending on the services included.
  • Surrogate compensation and expenses: typically €10,000 to €20,000 in total, covering a base allowance, maternity clothing, travel to appointments, and post-birth recovery support.
  • IVF and medical costs: typically €5,000 to €12,000 per cycle, inclusive of monitoring, egg retrieval where applicable, and embryo transfer.
  • Legal fees (Albanian counsel): typically €2,000 to €5,000, though complex parentage proceedings may cost more.
  • Home country legal costs: variable; budget for a separate legal process in your country of residence.
  • Contingency fund: it is prudent to hold a reserve of at least €5,000 to €10,000 to cover unexpected medical costs, additional legal hearings, or delays in exit documentation.

These figures are illustrative ranges drawn from general market knowledge and should not be treated as quotes. Obtain itemised written quotations from any agency or clinic before signing any agreement.

Practical Steps

Step 1: Initial Research and Legal Mapping

Before approaching any agency, commission an independent legal opinion from both an Albanian family lawyer and a lawyer in your home country. This dual assessment will clarify whether the arrangement is likely to be recognised domestically and will identify procedural requirements at both ends. Do not rely solely on guidance provided by an agency, as their interests may not be fully aligned with yours.

Step 2: Selecting an Agency and Clinic

There is no official accreditation body for surrogacy agencies in Albania as of writing. Carry out thorough due diligence on any agency you consider: request references, ask for evidence of successful completions, review contract terms carefully, and confirm that escrow arrangements are in place for surrogate payments. Our due diligence checklist outlines the questions you should ask at this stage — equivalent guidance can be found on our how it works page.

Step 3: Medical Assessment and IVF Protocol

Both intended parents and the prospective surrogate will undergo a series of medical assessments before an embryo transfer is attempted. These typically include hormonal profiling, uterine evaluation, infectious disease screening, and genetic counselling where donor material is involved. The number of IVF cycles required varies by individual circumstance, and no clinical outcome can be guaranteed.

Step 4: Legal Agreements

A surrogacy agreement should be drafted and reviewed by independent legal counsel for each party before any medical procedure takes place. As of writing, the enforceability of such contracts in Albanian courts has not been definitively tested, but a well-drafted agreement remains important as a record of intent and as a basis for any subsequent legal proceedings.

Step 5: Birth and Parentage Process

When the birth approaches, your legal team should have a clear procedural plan for registration and for obtaining the documentation needed to travel home with the child. This may involve a court application, a DNA declaration, and coordination with your home country's consulate or embassy. Allow for the possibility that this process may take several weeks.

Step 6: Returning Home

Contact your home country's embassy in Albania before the birth to understand what travel documents the child will require and whether any pre-approval is needed. Some countries require a parental order or adoption order before they will grant citizenship or a passport to a child born through surrogacy abroad. Failing to plan for this stage can result in extended stays in Albania and significant additional cost.

Key Considerations at a Glance

  • The legal framework is not codified and carries inherent uncertainty.
  • Home-country recognition is a separate legal question and must be investigated independently.
  • Costs are lower than many Western destinations but include multiple components beyond agency fees.
  • Agency due diligence is especially important in a lightly regulated environment.
  • Allow adequate time and budget for post-birth legal steps in both Albania and your home country.
